How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the row above. A ball dropped from the top has a near-even chance of moving left or right at every peg, repeating that split as it goes downward. After the last row, it lands in one of the pockets at the bottom, which is always one more than the number of rows.

Because each path is built from repeated fifty-fifty turns, balls cluster near the middle and only rarely reach the outer edges. Statisticians call this a Galton board, and the shape it creates is a binomial distribution. The payout table is the same curve turned upside down: common centre pockets pay less, while rare outer pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is set to sit near the inverse of its probability, then trimmed by a small house margin. When you multiply every pocket payout by the chance of reaching it, the total comes in slightly below one; that difference is the operator’s edge. Most published versions quote a return to player between about 97% and 99%, depending on the studio and the chosen settings. A sensible rule of thumb: no row count or risk level can shift the long-run return in your favour. The controls change variance — how uneven the ride is — not the average outcome.

Reputable releases use a certified random number generator for each deflection, and provably fair versions publish seed hashes so a round can be checked independently. What no version can do is let you steer the ball.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
A slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier from a finished ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines crossed by the ball; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or flattens the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small gap between true odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A method of published hashes that lets players check that a result was not altered.
Variance
The size of the swings around the average result — the part the risk switch really controls.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection is driven by an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you confirm later that the path was set before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge payouts become larger while their chance of landing falls. The overall return stays essentially the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically better. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value into rare edge hits. Choose based on how much swing your budget and temperament can handle.

Can I practise without spending anything?

Yes. The board on this page uses virtual credits, and most operators host a free mode with the same odds. The maths does not change when money is added.

Does timing or button rhythm influence where a ball lands?

No. The path is set by the random generator, not by when or how fast you press drop. Any pattern you see is coincidence mistaken for a system.
